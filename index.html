Interferometric Exoplanet Imager

What would a telescope array actually see of an Earth-like planet? The planet is ~1010× fainter than its star, so the answer depends on sharpness (wavelength ÷ array size) and on how many photons you collect.

How this works (and what it leaves out)
  • Same brightness scale on both images. White = the brightest part of the true planet. A blurred planet looks dimmer because its light is spread over more sky, as it would in a real image.
  • Sharpness. An array of size B at wavelength λ resolves details down to about λ/B. Earth is 2R/d across: ≈8.5 µas at 10 pc, so seeing any surface detail needs arrays hundreds of km wide.
  • Simple mode treats the array as one ideal filled mirror the size of the array (the best any array of that size could do). Its image is the planet blurred by an Airy pattern of width λ/B.
  • Advanced mode uses the actual telescope positions. The beams are combined like a hypertelescope (densified pupil). The image is the planet blurred by the array's real point-spread function: interference fringes from every telescope pair, including rotation, spectral channels and phasing errors. Those fringes sit inside an envelope about λ/(telescope spacing) wide, the "clean field". Each telescope also only sees ~λ/D of sky. Sparse arrays put much of the light into a halo around the sharp core, so detailed targets lose contrast unless you deconvolve.
  • Planet light. Reflected sunlight (geometric albedo 0.367, Lambert phase law) plus thermal emission from a temperature map (cold cloud tops, warm deserts, limb darkening). Planck spectra set the mix, so beyond ~4–5 µm you see the planet's heat, not sunlight.
  • Noise. Photons = flux × bandwidth × mirror area × throughput × time, with Poisson noise per pixel. Backgrounds are the star's diffraction glare after suppression (from each telescope's Airy envelope at the star–planet separation) and local zodiacal light.
  • Not modeled: detector read noise, exozodiacal dust, atmospheric turbulence beyond the phasing error, bandwidth smearing, the planet's rotation during long exposures, and its spectral bands.

The field of view is always 2.5× the planet's diameter, rendered on a 256×256 grid. Fringes finer than one pixel average out within the pixel.
